## 1. Sourced Definitions

### 1.1 ChartSchool (StockCharts.com, "Rectangle")

- **Touch minimum:** "At least two equivalent reaction highs" for the upper resistance line and "two equivalent reaction lows" for the lower support line. Touches "don't need to be exactly equal but should be reasonably close." Alternating highs/lows preferred, not mandatory.
- **Duration:** Patterns under **3 weeks** are "generally considered a flag," not a rectangle. Ideal development window is **~3 months**. Longer patterns produce more significant breakouts.
- **Breakout confirmation:** "For a breakout to be considered valid, it should be **on a closing basis**." Some traders additionally require a 3% move, 3-day hold, or volume expansion — noted as optional filters, not part of the core definition.
- **Character:** A "neutral pattern dependent on the direction of the future breakout" — usually a continuation pattern, occasionally marks a reversal. This is explicitly different from triangles, whose three sub-shapes (ascending/descending/symmetrical) each carry a directional lean by construction.
- **Volume:** Inconsistent — may decline, stay choppy, or rarely rise into the breakout. No reliable pre-breakout volume signature (unlike the symmetrical triangle's typical volume contraction).

Source: [Rectangle | ChartSchool](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/chart-analysis/chart-patterns/rectangle)

### 1.2 CMT-adjacent material

Substantively identical to ChartSchool on the core mechanics (2-touch minimum, parallel horizontal lines, continuation pattern formed during a trend pause, "buyers and sellers at equilibrium"). No CMT-specific numeric threshold beyond what ChartSchool already states was found in public material — the two sources converge rather than diverge here.

### 1.3 Aksel Kibar / @TechCharts — confirmed via direct fetch, not assumed

Three sources fetched directly (not summarized secondhand):

- **X post** (2021812604008579206): *"I look for these horizontal setups with minimum 3 touch points to the well-defined pattern boundary. Several touch points is not an indication that the stock is having difficulty to break that level, it is actually [a sign the market has recognized the level as a technical hurdle; breakouts after several tests are usually powerful and followed by strong trends]."* — **This is Kibar's explicit, rectangle-specific touch minimum: 3, not ChartSchool/CMT's 2.**
- **"RECTANGLE - HORIZONTAL CHART PATTERN"** (blog.techcharts.net, 2020-02-11) and **"LONG-TERM CHART PATTERNS"** (2020-08-20): *"I like to focus on chart patterns with horizontal boundaries. They are easier to identify on price charts and focusing on horizontal boundaries will remove the bias from your analysis."* Explicit caution against diagonal trend lines: *"it is much easier to think that there is a diagonal trend line somewhere (seeing trend lines that are not valid) on the price chart... it is relatively difficult to come up with a well-defined horizontal consolidation with price having several touches on the boundaries."* Elsewhere (search-surfaced, same voice/content as the blog): *"If you want to save yourself 2-3 years of back and forth while looking for the right strategy in financial markets: stop drawing diagonal lines, including trend lines."*

- **Measured-move target:** mentioned in every example (e.g., FANCL: resistance 3,225.0 → target 4,315.0) but the exact calculation is not spelled out in the fetched text — consistent with the standard classical-charting convention (rectangle height projected from the breakout point), not independently re-derived here. **Deferred to a later stage, not locked now** — see Section 5.

Sources: [Aksel Kibar, CMT on X](https://x.com/TechCharts/status/2021812604008579206) · [RECTANGLE - HORIZONTAL CHART PATTERN](https://blog.techcharts.net/index.php/2020/02/11/rectangle-horizontal-chart-pattern/) · [LONG-TERM CHART PATTERNS](https://blog.techcharts.net/2020/08/20/long-term-rectangles/) · [RECTANGLE CHART PATTERN BREAKOUTS](https://blog.techcharts.net/index.php/2020/01/21/rectangle-breakouts/)

## 2. Central Design Decision: Horizontal-Fit, Not Diagonal-Fit-That-Happens-To-Be-Flat

The task brief flagged this as the design question to decide explicitly, not default silently. Two paths were available:

**Path A (rejected): treat a rectangle as a degenerate triangle.** Reuse `fit_boundary()` (Theil-Sen + OLS free-slope fit) unchanged for both boundaries, then classify as "rectangle" if both fitted lines pass `classify_triangle()`'s existing flatness test (`upper_flat and lower_flat`, currently the one branch `classify_triangle()` maps to `None` — "not a triangle shape"). This would reuse the most code with zero new fitting logic.

**Path B (chosen): fit a genuine horizontal level directly**, not derive flatness as a byproduct of a free-slope fit. A new `fit_horizontal_level()` function (Section 3) pins slope at exactly 0 by construction and asks a different question than `fit_boundary()` does: not "what's the best-fit line through these points" but "what price level did the most pivots actually touch."

**Reasoning for Path B:**
1. It matches Kibar's own stated method — a rectangle boundary is *found* as a horizontal level with several touches, not *derived* from a free-slope fit that happens to come out near zero. These are different claims. A Theil-Sen/OLS fit with a small nonzero slope can satisfy `flat_cumulative_pct` (an aggregate, window-edge-to-edge measure) while never actually passing through 3 touches at one consistent price — the exact gap between "flat in aggregate" and "a well-defined level with several touches," which is precisely the distinction Kibar's own quote is making ("relatively difficult to come up with a well-defined horizontal consolidation... having several touches on the boundaries" — implying this is a different, harder bar than just not-obviously-sloped).
2. It avoids silently reconciling the two sources' touch-count disagreement (2 vs. 3). Path A would inherit `fit_boundary()`'s existing `min_touches` default of 2 as the path of least resistance; Path B forces the touch-count decision to be made explicitly for this pattern (Section 2 above: locked at 3).
3. `Boundary` (the dataclass) is reused unchanged either way — only the *fitting algorithm* differs (Section 3). This preserves 100% of the downstream reuse (`check_violation()`, `is_forced_line()`, `breakout_classification.py`, `stop_loss_definitions.py` — see Section 4) regardless of which path was chosen, so nothing downstream is put at risk by choosing B over A.

**Status at first writing: presented as a recommendation requiring confirmation, not a unilateral choice** — the alternative (Path A) is not unreasonable and reuses more existing code; it is rejected here specifically because it doesn't match Kibar's stated method as precisely, and this study's discipline is to align with his stated preference where the two sources diverge (per the task brief). **Superseded by the reconsideration in Section 2.1 below.**

**Consequence: no separate "flatness classification" step is needed for rectangles**, unlike triangles' three-iteration flatness work (Triangle spec Section 3.4). Triangles needed post-hoc flatness classification because their boundaries come from a free diagonal fit and the *shape* had to be judged afterward. A rectangle's boundary is horizontal by construction under Path B — flatness is enforced at fit time via the tolerance band, not classified after the fact. This is reported as a finding, not assumed: rectangles are structurally simpler to classify than triangles specifically because the pattern's defining shape constraint is imposed at detection time rather than inferred post-hoc.

### 2.1 Reconsidered and Reaffirmed Under Adaptive Windowing — CONFIRMED 2026-07-16

Once Q6 was resolved (Section 3.2: adaptive, pivot-anchored, breakout-closing growing window), Q1's original reasoning was re-derived rather than assumed to carry over unchanged — the original argument above was framed entirely around a *fixed* window, where both edges are arbitrary scan artifacts with no necessary relationship to the pattern.

**The anchored start partially, not fully, weakens the original argument.** Under adaptive windowing, the window's start is now a real pivot — by construction, the first bar is already a genuine touch on the level being built, which the original fixed-window framing didn't have. This is a real mitigation of the original concern (a free-slope fit accidentally satisfying an aggregate flatness threshold without genuine multi-point clustering) — but only a partial one: nothing about anchoring the start guarantees the *interior* pivots cluster near a consistent price, and the window's end (the breakout bar) isn't a touch at all, so a free-slope fit could still thread a small-slope line from the one genuine start-touch through non-clustered interior points. The core mechanism — aggregate edge-to-edge drift vs. genuine multi-point clustering — is a property of the flatness metric itself, not of how the window's edges were chosen, and persists at reduced but nonzero strength. **On reconsideration, the original aggregate-flatness argument was somewhat overstated when evaluated against an anchored-start window — real, but weaker than originally presented.**

**A new, stronger argument specific to adaptive growth: incremental-fit stability.** Adaptive growth requires evaluating boundary validity incrementally as new pivots arrive — a requirement fixed-window triangles never faced, since `fit_boundary()` was always run once on a complete, fixed candidate set. Under Path A, re-running or incrementally updating a free-slope Theil-Sen/OLS fit as the window grows means the *fitted slope itself can drift* with each new point, since slope is a free parameter continually re-estimated on more data. A boundary that passes the flatness check at 3 touches could re-fit to a materially non-flat slope once a 4th or 5th pivot arrives, forcing retroactive invalidation of an already-accepted window mid-growth — a failure mode with no clean resolution (roll back the window? freeze the fit at first acceptance, defeating the point of re-checking flatness at all?) and no analog under triangles' one-shot fixed-window fit. A considered alternative — refit from scratch at every growth step instead of updating incrementally — does not help: it reintroduces the original risk (each fresh fit could pass flatness by coincidence of whatever the current window's edges happen to be) at strictly worse cost (full O(k²) Theil-Sen pairwise-slope recomputation on every pivot added, vs. once per fixed window for triangles).

Path B has no equivalent problem. Once a level is established from ≥3 inliers, it has no slope to drift — the only thing that can move is the price level itself, bounded within `tolerance_pct` by the median refit. Checking whether a new pivot keeps the window valid is a cheap, stable, O(1) "is this point within tolerance of the already-fixed level" test, never a re-estimation that can retroactively reverse a prior acceptance.

**Net conclusion — Path B reaffirmed, but the PRIMARY justification changes.** The decisive argument is now incremental-growth stability, not the original aggregate-flatness concern alone: the aggregate-flatness argument is real but only partially applicable once the window is anchor-started; the incremental-growth argument is decisive independent of how the window's edges were chosen, and applies with full force specifically because Q6 resolved to adaptive growth over a fixed scan. This is a reaffirmation reached by re-deriving the reasoning against the confirmed Q6 design, not a restatement of the original conclusion.

## 3. Math Spec: `fit_horizontal_level()`

Proposed signature (design only, not implemented):

```
fit_horizontal_level(pivots, tolerance_pct=0.015, min_touches=3) -> Optional[Boundary]
```

Input: a list of same-kind `Pivot` objects (all highs, for resistance; all lows, for support) from `pivots.find_pivots_collapsed()`, already restricted to the candidate window.

**Anchored vs. non-anchored boundary — confirmed asymmetry (2026-07-16, resolved alongside Q2/Q3, Section 6).** Under the confirmed adaptive windowing design (Section 3.2), exactly one boundary is anchored — its first pivot IS the window's anchor pivot, fixed by construction, not chosen from a candidate set. The other boundary has no anchor and must build its level organically from same-kind pivots arriving as the window grows. The two boundaries therefore run different versions of the procedure below:

- **Anchored boundary:** steps 1-3 do not apply — there is only ever one candidate (the anchor pivot's price), never a set to select from. The procedure starts at step 4 (refit as new same-kind pivots accumulate) using the anchor as the seed.
- **Non-anchored boundary:** the full procedure below applies, including the tie-break at step 3.

Procedure (non-anchored boundary; anchored boundary skips to step 4 seeded by the anchor pivot):
1. For each pivot `p_i`, treat its price as a candidate level `L = p_i.price`.
2. Inlier set for candidate `L`: every pivot `p_j` with `|p_j.price - L| <= L * tolerance_pct` — same tolerance-band convention as `fit_boundary()` (percentage-of-price, not ATR), for consistency with the rest of the pipeline.
3. Select the candidate `L` with the largest inlier set. **Tie-break rule — CONFIRMED 2026-07-16: tightest inlier price spread** (mirrors `boundaries.py`'s own residual-minimizing spirit), chosen over earliest-index pivot specifically because adaptive growth should let accumulating evidence determine the level, not lock onto whichever pivot happened to arrive first chronologically — the opposite bias from what the anchor's own construction already provides on the other boundary. This only matters in the early-growth phase, before one candidate's inlier-count dominance becomes clear from a larger pivot count; it does not apply to the anchored boundary at all (see above — resolves what was originally posed as a single Q3 into two structurally different cases, only one of which was ever a genuine open question).
4. Refit the level as the median of the winning inlier set's prices (a location-only refit — no slope to re-derive, so this is a single recompute, not `fit_boundary()`'s iterate-to-convergence loop).
5. Reclassify inliers against the refit level once more (the median may shift slightly after step 4) and finalize.
6. If the final inlier count is `< min_touches`: return `None` — an honest absence of a validated level, matching `fit_boundary()`'s own "don't fabricate a fit" discipline (Triangle spec Section 3.2). **`min_touches` — CONFIRMED 2026-07-16: 3 remains the default/primary-reported value** (unchanged rationale: aligning with Kibar's stated preference where the two sources diverge over ChartSchool/CMT's 2), **but `min_touches=2` is run as a co-equal comparison on the first real-data pass, not a fallback** — resolving Q2 required surfacing a genuine asymmetry the earlier reasoning-only analysis couldn't settle: the anchored boundary gets its first touch for free from construction, while the non-anchored boundary must accumulate all `min_touches` organically inside the same short 21-60 day window (Section 3.2), with no equivalent head start. Whether 3 touches are realistically reachable on the non-anchored boundary within that window is an empirical question, not a reasoning one — hence the dual-run rather than a forced single default.
7. Return `Boundary(slope=0.0, intercept=refit_level, touch_indices=[inlier indices], all_candidate_indices=[all pivot indices in window])` — **the existing `Boundary` dataclass, unmodified.** `price_at(index)` on a slope-0 boundary correctly returns the constant level for any index, so every downstream consumer of a `Boundary` needs zero awareness that this came from a different fitting algorithm than `fit_boundary()`.

### 3.1 `assemble_rectangle()` — proposed checks, in order

Analog to `assemble_triangle()`, but rectangle-specific:

1. `fit_horizontal_level()` succeeds for both pivot-highs (→ upper) and pivot-lows (→ lower) in the window; both required ≥ `min_touches` (3, default — run alongside `min_touches=2` as a co-equal comparison on the first real-data pass; Section 3, Section 6 Q2).
2. `is_forced_line(upper, ...)` / `is_forced_line(lower, ...)` — **reused unchanged** (Section 4.2 confirms why).
3. **New, rectangle-specific: `upper.intercept > lower.intercept`.** Triangles never need this check — converging lines cross by definition, so "which one is on top" is only ambiguous past the apex. Two independently-fit horizontal levels have no such guarantee; pivot noise could in principle produce an inverted or coincident pair, and this must be caught explicitly, not assumed away.
4. **New, rectangle-specific: minimum height.** `(upper.intercept - lower.intercept) / lower.intercept >= min_height_pct`. Guards against a degenerate near-zero-height "rectangle" — most concretely, the frozen-close artifact from Triangle spec Section 3.1 (TOWL/ISIL), where a pegged close could in principle let both a spurious "resistance" and a spurious "support" land within tolerance of nearly the same price. No convergence check exists for rectangles (parallel lines never converge) to catch this the way `compute_apex()` incidentally does for triangles, so this must be an explicit, independent guard. **`min_height_pct` — CONFIRMED 2026-07-16, derived not hardcoded (Section 6, Q5):** `min_height_pct = margin_multiplier * (2 * tolerance_pct / (1 - tolerance_pct))`, `margin_multiplier = 2.0` → `≈ 0.061` (~6.1%) at `tolerance_pct = 0.015`. Implement as the formula, not the literal `0.061`, so this stays consistent if `tolerance_pct` is revised.
5. No apex/convergence check — not applicable by construction (parallel boundaries). If a rectangle candidate were mistakenly run through `triangle.compute_apex()`, it would correctly return `None` ("parallel... a channel, not a converging triangle") — a sanity-check side effect worth noting, not something this pipeline needs to call.
6. Duration: calendar days from `start_date` to `end_date`, positive, **and** `>= MIN_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS` (21, confirmed — Section 3.2). Under the confirmed adaptive windowing design, the window itself never grows past `MAX_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS` (60 primary, Section 3.2) — a candidate that reaches the cap without breaking out closes there and is recorded as "expired without breakout," rather than being filtered out post hoc by this check.
7. No "kind" classification return value — see Section 4.3.

### 3.2 Candidate Window Generation — CONFIRMED (Q6, resolved 2026-07-16)

Calibration input from the Independent Quantitative Reviewer, grounded in real PSX trading experience: a "normal" PSX rectangle/base runs **no more than a couple of months** before breaking out or being abandoned — treated as more relevant than Kibar's own stated 6-month-to-5-year range, which reflects his own (likely more liquid, slower-moving) markets, not PSX specifically.

**Re-derived rejection of triangles' fixed 80-bar/50%-overlap scan — sharper than the original reasoning.** The original open-question note (Section 6, old Q6 text) rejected reusing triangles' windowing on the grounds that 80 bars is far too short for Kibar's 6mo–5yr range. Re-checked directly against the 2-month PSX-specific ceiling instead of Kibar's range: 80 bars ≈ 3.8 calendar months for a fully liquid, gap-free PSX name (~21 trading days/month) — actually in the same rough order of magnitude as "a couple of months," not obviously wrong the way it looked against Kibar's range. But this only holds for the most liquid tier. For mid/thin-tier names (70-89% / <70% trading-day coverage, per the triangle study's own liquidity tiering — e.g. `UPFL` at 42%), the same 80 bars spans 6-9+ calendar months — well past the 2-month ceiling. **Conclusion: reuse is still rejected, but for a sharper, PSX-specific reason than before** — not "80 bars is the wrong order of magnitude," but "a fixed bar-count window is inherently unstable against calendar time on this specific universe," which is the identical instability that already justified `triangle.py`'s calendar-day (not bar-count) duration field (Triangle spec Section 3, point 3). This is the same argument, now shown to apply to the *scanning* mechanism itself, not only to the *reported* duration field after the fact. Resolution: **define the candidate window in calendar days directly, not bar count.**

**Fixed vs. adaptive window — adaptive chosen.** Triangles' fixed-width sliding window is tolerable for triangles because triangles have a self-bounding geometric property rectangles lack: the apex. A triangle candidate framed at an arbitrary scan offset still gets checked against convergence/apex-distance/shape, so a bad framing is self-correcting — the geometry itself rejects it (`apex_behind_or_none`, `apex_too_far` were real, non-trivial rejection categories in the triangle results). Two parallel horizontal levels never converge and have no equivalent backstop. This creates a concrete failure mode unique to fixed-width scanning of rectangles: a genuine 45-day rectangle starting 20 days into a 60-day/30-day-step window would be split across two overlapping windows, each capturing only part of its touches — potentially failing the 3-touch minimum in both, despite the full pattern clearing it. Triangles were not exposed to this because triangle geometry doesn't depend on where you slice it; a rectangle's touch count is far more sensitive to exactly where the window boundary falls.

**Confirmed design: adaptive, pivot-anchored, breakout-closing growing window.**
1. **Anchor:** candidate starts are pivot locations from `find_pivots_collapsed()`, not arbitrary calendar offsets — every candidate begins at a genuine local extreme, which is itself the first real touch on the level being built.
2. **Grow:** the window extends forward as long as `fit_horizontal_level()` continues to find valid upper/lower levels and no close-based breach of either has occurred.
3. **Close on breakout:** the window ends the moment a genuine close-based breach occurs (reusing `check_violation()`'s existing close-based convention, unchanged) — this becomes the candidate's `end_date` directly, unifying pattern detection and breakout-occurrence into one pass, rather than triangles' two-stage approach (classify, then separately compute a breakout-occurrence horizon afterward).
4. **Close on cap** if no breakout occurs by the maximum duration — recorded as its own distinct outcome ("expired without breakout"), not dropped, matching the "0/545 → 2/545 never broke out" reporting discipline already established for triangles.

This maps cleanly onto the Reviewer's own phrasing — "breaks out or is abandoned" corresponds directly to "closes on breakout" vs. "closes on cap" as two structurally distinct exit conditions, not one blended duration threshold.

**Confirmed numbers (proposals adopted, not yet empirically validated):**
- `MIN_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS = 21` — ChartSchool's flag/rectangle cutoff, reused directly. This is the stronger of the two numbers: it is *sourced*, not estimated, and nothing in the PSX-specific ceiling input speaks to the floor.
- `MAX_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS = 60` (primary) — a literal reading of "a couple of months." **`90` is flagged as a fallback to test empirically**, not a competing default: a hard 60-day cap risks starving genuine thin-tier candidates (documented trading-day-coverage concern, same character as `UPFL`) of enough calendar time to ever accumulate 3 touches, even when a real pattern is present. Whether this actually happens is a question for the real-data stage, not resolved here — 90 days is the first thing to check if 60 yields suspiciously few thin-tier candidates.

**New interactions this surfaces, beyond the original Q6→Q2/Q4/Q5 notes** (window *length* affecting touch-count and height calibration): the fixed-vs-adaptive choice adds a second, separate axis.
- **→ Q2 (touch minimum):** independent of duration length, an adaptive window that grows exactly as far as the real pattern extends preserves more of a genuine pattern's true touch count than a fixed-width scan that risks clipping the pattern's tail or splitting it across two slices (the concrete failure mode above). This affects how achievable Kibar's 3-touch minimum is in practice, via a mechanism the original analysis didn't consider.
- **→ Q5 (`min_height_pct`):** an adaptive window's height is measured between the pattern's actual first-touch and breakout points, not a scan's arbitrary slice — likely making the height measurement less noisy (no risk of a fixed slice spanning a mid-pattern regime shift and spuriously inflating apparent range, or catching the pattern before its true range has developed).
- **→ Q4 (`tolerance_pct`):** no new interaction identified — tolerance is a price-band question, largely orthogonal to how the time-window is constructed.

**Not resolved by this decision, flagged for a later design pass, not a new numbered open question:** which pivots become anchor points (every pivot? highs and lows both? some subset?), and whether overlapping candidates sharing most of their growth history need deduplication.

## 4. Reuse Audit Against Triangle-Study Infrastructure

Per the task brief's instruction to confirm, not assume, each piece of reusable infrastructure.

### 4.1 `pivots.py` — confirmed reusable unchanged

`find_pivots_collapsed()` (fractal, `left=3, right=3`, frozen-close collapsing) is not triangle-specific — the frozen-close artifact it fixes (TOWL/ISIL circuit-lock plateaus) is a general PSX data property that applies to any pattern study on this universe, rectangles included. **Confirmed reusable as-is.**

One point flagged for empirical verification once real data is run (not resolved here, per this study's own discipline of finding things via real data, not assumption): rectangles are expected to run materially longer than triangles (Section 1.4), so a `left=3,right=3` fractal on a multi-month-to-multi-year window will likely surface many more interior pivots than a triangle's shorter window does. This is not expected to be a problem — `fit_horizontal_level()`'s clustering-by-price approach (Section 3) naturally ignores interior pivots that don't land within tolerance of the dominant level, regardless of how many there are — but it is flagged as something to confirm against real PSX output before treating pivot density as a non-issue, consistent with how every triangle-study finding was confirmed via real charts, not assumed from the code's logic alone.

### 4.2 `boundaries.py` — partially reusable, confirmed piece-by-piece

- **`Boundary` dataclass:** reused unchanged (Section 3, step 7).
- **`fit_boundary()`:** **not reused** for rectangle boundary detection — this is the Section 2 decision. (It remains available/correct for its original triangle use; nothing about this rejects `fit_boundary()` itself, only its applicability to rectangles specifically.)
- **`check_violation()`:** reused unchanged. It operates on any `Boundary` via `price_at(index)`, has no slope assumption, and its close-based breach convention already matches both ChartSchool's and Kibar's explicit close-based breakout confirmation (Section 1.4) — no adjustment needed, confirmed by direct comparison rather than assumed to transfer.

### 4.3 `triangle.py` — partially reusable, confirmed piece-by-piece

- **`is_forced_line()`:** reused unchanged. It reads only `boundary.touch_indices`, `window_start_index`, `window_end_index` — no dependency on slope anywhere in its logic — so the touch-spacing/forced-line check (ChartSchool's ["Trend Lines"](Triangle_Pattern_Specification_v1.md) touch-spacing rule, already validated as the dominant filter in the triangle study at 71-74% rejection) applies identically to a horizontal boundary. Confirmed by reading the function body, not assumed from its name.
- **`compute_apex()` / `classify_triangle()`:** **not reused.** Rectangles have no apex (parallel lines) and no shape sub-classification. This is a genuine, reportable contrast with triangles, not a gap: ChartSchool explicitly calls the rectangle a "neutral pattern" with no directional lean baked into its geometry, unlike triangles' ascending/descending/symmetrical split, each of which does carry a directional lean by construction. A rectangle candidate is a single shape; direction is unknowable until the breakout occurs. Consequence: whatever assembles a `RectangleCandidate` returns no `kind` field at all — there is nothing analogous to fill it with.
- **Calendar-day duration convention:** reused unchanged. Same PSX-gap rationale as triangles (Triangle spec Section 3, point 3) — bar-index spacing is not a safe duration proxy here either, arguably more so given rectangles' longer typical duration means more opportunity to cross a PSX data gap.

### 4.4 `breakout_classification.py` and `stop_loss_definitions.py` — confirmed reusable as-is, no modification needed

Both modules were audited by reading their actual function signatures, not assumed from the task brief's characterization:

- `classify_breakout()` takes a `Boundary`, `highs`/`lows`, `breakout_idx`, `direction`, `horizon_end` — nothing triangle-specific. Since `assemble_rectangle()` (Section 3.1) returns real `Boundary` instances (slope=0 is just a valid value), this module needs **zero changes** to classify a rectangle breakout's Type 1-4 outcome.
- `raw_lfd_level()` / `walk_forward()` in `stop_loss_definitions.py` operate on `highs`/`lows`/`closes`/`breakout_idx`/`direction` directly, with no `Boundary` dependency at all for the LFD side, and only a plain `float` `opposite_stop` price for the optional third arm — trivially satisfiable by `boundary.price_at(breakout_idx)` on either a triangle or rectangle boundary. **Zero changes needed.**

## 6. Open Questions Requiring Explicit Confirmation Before Any Code Is Written

1. ~~Horizontal-fit vs. degenerate-triangle path (Section 2).~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16 — see Section 2.1.** Path B (dedicated `fit_horizontal_level()`, slope pinned at 0) reaffirmed under the confirmed adaptive-windowing design (Q6 / Section 3.2), but the reasoning was re-derived, not carried over unchanged. Primary justification is now **incremental-growth stability**: a free-slope fit's slope can drift as new pivots arrive during adaptive growth, forcing retroactive invalidation of an already-accepted window — a pinned-horizontal level cannot drift this way, since it has no slope to re-estimate. This is a stronger, adaptive-growth-specific argument than the original aggregate-flatness concern, which reconsideration showed is only *partially* mitigated (not resolved) by anchoring the window's start to a real pivot.
2. ~~Touch minimum: 3 (Kibar) vs. 2 (ChartSchool/CMT).~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16.** 3 remains the default/primary-reported value — unchanged rationale, aligning with Kibar's stated preference where the two sources diverge. What changed is *how* this gets tested: reconsidering Q2 against the confirmed adaptive design (Section 3.2, Section 2.1) surfaced a genuine boundary asymmetry not visible in the original reasoning-only analysis — the anchor pivot supplies one boundary's first touch for free (by construction), while the *other* boundary must accumulate all `min_touches` organically inside the same short 21-60 day window, with no equivalent head start. Whether 3 touches are realistically reachable on the non-anchored boundary in that window is an empirical question. **Confirmed protocol: run `min_touches=3` and `min_touches=2` as a co-equal comparison on the first real-data pass — not a primary-plus-fallback.** `min_touches` remains caller-supplied either way.
3. ~~Tie-break rule for `fit_horizontal_level()`'s candidate-level selection (Section 3, step 3).~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16, and reframed in the process.** The anchored boundary has no tie-break question at all — its only candidate is the anchor pivot's price, by construction, from the first bar onward; nothing to select among. The question only ever genuinely applied to the non-anchored boundary's early-growth phase, before enough same-kind pivots accumulate for one candidate level's inlier-count dominance to become clear. **Confirmed rule: tightest inlier price spread** (mirrors `boundaries.py`'s residual-minimizing precedent), chosen over earliest-index pivot because adaptive growth should let accumulating evidence determine the level as the window matures, not lock onto whichever pivot happened to arrive first.
4. ~~`tolerance_pct` for rectangle touches.~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16.** Reuse `0.015` (1.5%) unchanged. Confirmed that adaptive vs. fixed windowing (Q6) has no bearing on this calibration — `tolerance_pct` is a pure price-magnitude test (`tol = L * tolerance_pct`), independent of time or how the window's edges were determined; an incremental-few-touches concern was considered and rejected as adaptive-specific, since it applies equally to a fixed window evaluated with few pivots. The one real, unresolved uncertainty is duration-driven, not windowing-mechanism-driven, and is direction-ambiguous from reasoning alone: 21-60 day windows (vs. triangles' ~4-month de-facto windows) could argue for *tighter* tolerance (less time for legitimate drift) or *wider* tolerance (if short-resolving PSX rectangles form in choppier regimes than Kibar's longer, quieter bases) — flagged for real-data validation, watching specifically for genuine touches on near-21-day candidates being wrongly rejected (too tight) vs. spurious touches inflating counts and destabilizing the Q5 height check (too loose).
5. ~~`min_height_pct` (Section 3.1, check 4).~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16 — derived, not borrowed.** For the upper and lower tolerance bands to not overlap at the minimum allowed height, `height_pct > 2 * tolerance_pct / (1 - tolerance_pct)` — a mechanical floor of **~3.05%** at `tolerance_pct = 0.015`. This makes the original placeholder (`0.03`, borrowed from `triangle.py`'s unrelated `flat_cumulative_pct`) a genuine finding, not just an unvalidated guess: 3.00% sits *below* this floor, meaning it would have shipped as internally inconsistent — the two tolerance bands essentially touching, with no real separation, at exactly the minimum height the check is supposed to protect. **Confirmed formula, not a hardcoded constant:** `min_height_pct = margin_multiplier * (2 * tolerance_pct / (1 - tolerance_pct))`, with `margin_multiplier = 2.0` as the starting proposal → `min_height_pct ≈ 0.061` (~6.1%). Deriving it this way keeps `min_height_pct` automatically consistent if `tolerance_pct` is later revised during real-data validation (Q4), rather than requiring the two independent placeholders to be manually re-checked against each other every time one changes. **Confirmed flat, not duration-scaled:** the failure mode this guards against (near-coincident levels, the frozen-close-artifact false-level risk) is a price-magnitude property, not a time one — a genuine rectangle formed in 21 days is exactly as valid as one formed in 58, so nothing about realized duration should change the minimum required separation. `margin_multiplier = 2.0` itself is flagged for real-data confirmation, not locked: the mechanical floor (~3.05%) is mathematically solid, but whether 2x margin above it is the right amount — versus real PSX rectangles, especially on thinner/less volatile names, genuinely clustering at 4-5% heights a 6.1% floor would wrongly reject — is an empirical question, the same way triangle flatness took three iterations against real KEL data before landing.
6. ~~`MIN_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS` (Section 3.1, check 6).~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-16 — see Section 3.2.** Confirmed: adaptive, pivot-anchored, breakout-closing growing window (not a fixed-width scan, and not triangles' bar-count-based scan). `MIN_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS = 21` (ChartSchool). `MAX_RECTANGLE_DURATION_DAYS = 60` primary, `90` flagged as an empirical fallback if 60 starves thin-tier candidates. Calibration input: PSX rectangles run "no more than a couple of months," per the Independent Quantitative Reviewer's direct trading experience — treated as more relevant than Kibar's 6mo–5yr range, which reflects his own, likely more liquid, markets. Numbers remain proposals pending real-data validation, consistent with how every other number in this document is handled — but the *structural* decision (adaptive vs. fixed) is locked.
